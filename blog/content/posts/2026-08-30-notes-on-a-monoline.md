A monoline typeface promises simplicity: one stroke width, everywhere. The promise is a lie, and the craft is in how gracefully you break it.

## Where the weight hides

Look at any well-made monoline at text sizes and you'll find the joins are thinned, the bowls are slightly heavier than the stems, and the round letters overshoot the flat ones. The eye reads *even*; the outline is anything but.

> Optical evenness is a negotiation between geometry and the retina, and the retina always wins.

Three places to look:

1. **Joins.** Where a curve meets a stem the ink pools. Cut it back.
2. **Horizontals.** They read heavier than verticals at equal width. Trim 5–8%.
3. **Overshoot.** Rounds and points must extend past the baseline and cap height or they look short.

## Inclusive Sans

Olivia King's [Inclusive Sans](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/Inclusive+Sans) is a monoline that takes legibility research seriously without turning into a dyslexia-font caricature. The `a` is double-storey, the `l` has a tail, the `I` has serifs, the `0` is not the `O`. Those decisions cost a little rhythm and buy a lot of clarity, which is the right trade for a UI or a dashboard.

This site's **Paper** theme uses it at 300 for display and 400 for text. If you switch to **Gallery** you'll get Instrument Sans instead: a grotesk with more contrast between letters and a sharper personality. Both are monoline in spirit. Neither is monoline in fact.

## Spacing beats shapes

If you only have an afternoon, spend it on spacing, not on outlines. A mediocre alphabet with good sidebearings sets better than a beautiful one set badly. Kern last, and kern less than you think.
